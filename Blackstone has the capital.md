## The Firms Behind the Announcement

What gets built during an enterprise AI engagement is not just a software integration. It is a map of how a company actually operates. The prompts, the workflow logic, the schema mappings, the evaluation criteria, the internal data connections. A 2026 analysis of AI vendor relationships found that lock-in in these engagements most commonly comes from undocumented workflow dependencies and vendor-specific implementation layers, not from the model itself.

When a company outsources its design function to an external supplier, the supplier ends up holding all the design knowledge. The client retains nothing proprietary. Anyone with access to the same supplier can replicate the arrangement. The client has not built an asset. The client has built a dependency.

This is not a new dynamic. It has been documented across manufacturing, pharmaceutical distribution, and platform-based marketing for decades. What changes when AI is the delivery mechanism is the depth and speed at which the dependency forms.

Platform-based marketing infrastructure offers a useful reference point. When a business builds its content strategy, lead tracking, and revenue reporting inside a single platform, that platform develops visibility into the company's operations before the company itself has processed the same information. Migration away from it becomes structurally costly not because the contract prevents it but because the operational knowledge is now distributed across a system the client does not own.

Bespoke AI deployments work the same way at higher velocity. The implementation partner learns the company's decision logic, its exception handling, its tolerance thresholds, its internal language. That knowledge gets encoded into a system architecture the client depends on to function. When the engagement ends or the contract renews, the client is not in the same negotiating position it started in.

Pharmaceutical incumbents retained bargaining power during the biotechnology disruption of the 1980s and 1990s not because of superior science. They retained it because they controlled the organizational and complementary assets that new entrants could not easily replicate. Manufacturing relationships, regulatory navigation, distribution infrastructure. Toward the end of that cycle, control of delivery infrastructure outweighed control of the underlying ideas. The firms structuring these AI service agreements understand this pattern.

## The Term Sheet

Each of these represents operational knowledge that moves from the client to the implementation partner during deployment. The contract language governing what happens to that knowledge after the engagement is the most consequential document in the arrangement.

- Custom prompt libraries built during deployment
- Workflow logic encoding the client's internal decision rules
- Schema mappings connecting AI outputs to operational systems
- Evaluation harnesses calibrated to the client's specific standards
- Data pipelines the client cannot easily reconstruct independently

Current public guidance on AI contracts identifies data use limits, IP provisions, retention rules, and service descriptions as the primary safeguards. Most mid-sized companies entering these arrangements do not have the legal infrastructure to negotiate those terms with precision against counterparties of this size and sophistication.

The workforce dimension of this is slower moving and harder to see while it is happening. When a team stops performing a function because a system handles it, the skill does not stay dormant. It atrophies. The institutional knowledge of how to do the thing, how to recognize when it is being done incorrectly, how to intervene when the system produces a wrong answer, quietly leaves the organization. Not through any single decision. Through accumulation.

Research on automation and labor displacement going back to the 1950s documents this pattern consistently. Machines replace human judgment in a specific domain. The workforce adjusts. The adjustment looks like efficiency. Over a long enough timeline it looks like an organization that can no longer diagnose its own processes.

In healthcare this is not an abstraction. It is a documented and accelerating risk. AI diagnostic systems are trained on fixed data snapshots. As disease patterns shift, as patient demographics change, as imaging hardware is updated, the model continues running on its original training. Researchers have named this the zombie algorithm problem. The system produces outputs. The outputs are no longer reliably calibrated to current conditions. Clinical staff who have restructured their workflows around the system's outputs may not have the independent diagnostic depth to catch the drift.

Healthcare already operates with what one 2025 analysis described as an unhealthy reliance on single vendors for critical technology. The Oracle Cloud breach in early 2025, which affected the EMR systems of multiple hospital systems, demonstrated what happens when a single vendor relationship fails at scale. Denied or delayed care. Increases in patient morbidity and mortality tied directly to system unavailability.

Between 2022 and 2025 the number of FDA authorized AI medical devices nearly doubled. Analysis of approximately 950 cleared devices through 2024 found that 96.7 percent were cleared through the 510k pathway, which requires only a demonstration of substantial equivalence rather than rigorous clinical trial evidence. The evidence base for what happens when these systems fail at scale does not yet exist. The deployments are already running.

Insufficient governance of AI was named the second highest patient safety threat in 2025 by ECRI. The challenge they identified was not technical. Policy cannot adapt as fast as the technology moves. By the time governance catches up, the dependency is already structural.
